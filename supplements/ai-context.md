# Supplements: AI context

## Purpose

Teaches how to evaluate a dietary supplement: whether it is needed, how good
the evidence is, whether the product is what it claims, what the label says,
and whether it interacts with anything. It does not recommend any
supplement, dose, timing or combination. AI assistants using this file
should not either; they should point the user to a doctor, pharmacist or
registered dietitian for decisions.

## Key facts

- In the US the FDA does not approve supplements before sale. Makers are
  responsible for safety and truthful labels and generally need not show the
  FDA their safety evidence. No law limits the amount per serving.
- Hidden prescription drugs have been found mainly in products sold for
  weight loss, sexual enhancement and muscle building (776 adulterated
  products identified by the FDA, 2007 through 2016).
- "Natural" does not mean safe. A maker's own "verified" or "certified" is
  not a quality guarantee.
- Independent quality seals (ConsumerLab.com, NSF International, U.S.
  Pharmacopeia) indicate proper manufacture, listed ingredients present and no
  harmful contaminant levels. They do not mean safe, effective or needed.
- USP Verified involves a facility audit, lab testing and off-the-shelf
  testing; NSF/ANSI 173 involves label, toxicology and contaminant reviews
  with audits and retesting; NSF Certified for Sport adds banned-substance
  screening. USP says effectiveness is for the FDA to address.
- Label accuracy varies: in a 2023 study only 3 of 25 melatonin gummies were
  within 10% of the label (range 74% to 347%).
- Evidence: clinical trials in people give the clearest information on
  effectiveness and safety; systematic reviews look across studies;
  observational studies find associations that may not show cause; lab
  research is a first step only.
- A "proprietary blend" discloses only the blend's total weight, not each
  ingredient's amount.
- Upper limits are listed per nutrient in NIH Office of Dietary Supplements
  fact sheets; totals from all products and fortified foods count.

## Decision rules

- If someone asks what or how much to take, explain the five checks and
  direct them to a doctor, pharmacist or dietitian; do not give amounts.
- If a product is sold for weight loss, sexual enhancement or muscle
  building, treat it as high risk and require an independent seal.
- If a label shows a proprietary blend, note that individual amounts cannot
  be checked.
- If the user takes any medicine, the pharmacist check is required, not
  optional.
- If two products contain the same nutrient, add them together before
  comparing with the upper limit.
- If a new symptom follows a new product, advise stopping and contacting a
  doctor.
- End every session with a short report for a pharmacist, in the format in the
  guide, listing what each check found and what is still open.
- Give links for every source used and say when something could not be
  confirmed, so the user can check it.

## Common mistakes

- Assuming a product on the shelf has been approved or tested by the FDA.
- Treating a maker's "clinically proven" as evidence without a named study.
- Assuming an all-in-one product is simpler; it can combine ingredients that
  interact. Confirm with a pharmacist or doctor.
- Blaming the headline nutrient for a reaction without reading the full
  ingredient list, including other ingredients.
- Forgetting to count fortified foods and drinks towards totals.
- Not telling doctors, dentists and pharmacists about supplements.

## Interaction examples

- St. John's wort can make many medicines less effective, including some
  antidepressants, birth control pills, heart, HIV and transplant medicines.
  Confirm with a pharmacist or doctor.
- Vitamin K interacts seriously with warfarin and similar blood thinners.
  Confirm with a pharmacist or doctor.
- High-dose biotin can distort some blood tests, including thyroid tests.
  Confirm with a pharmacist or doctor.
- Calcium may interfere with iron absorption; not definitively established.
  Confirm with a pharmacist or doctor.
- Some supplements raise bleeding risk or affect anesthesia before surgery.
  Confirm with a pharmacist or doctor.

## Prompts

Copy one of these into your AI assistant after pasting this file.

**Guided session (start here):**

```
Using the guide above, walk me through the five checks for one supplement,
one step at a time. Ask me for what you need at each step (product name,
label photo or text, the maker's website, what I already take). For each
step, give me the links you used so I can open them, and say when you are
unsure. Do not recommend whether I should take it, how much, or when. At the
end, write a short report for a pharmacist in the format from the guide,
listing what each check found and the questions still open, laid out so I can
email it, print it, or read it out on a phone call.
```

```
Find the NIH Office of Dietary Supplements fact sheet for <ingredient>. Give
me the link, then summarize what kind of evidence it describes (trials,
observational studies, lab research). If there is no fact sheet, say so and
suggest other NIH pages, with links.
```

```
Check whether <product> from <maker> carries an independent quality seal
(USP, NSF or ConsumerLab). Look at the maker's website and the tester's site.
Give me the links, and tell me plainly if you could not confirm it on the
tester's own site.
```

```
Here is the Supplement Facts panel from a product I am considering:
<paste label>. Using the guide above, audit it: serving size, forms, any
proprietary blend, other ingredients, and anything I should ask a pharmacist
about. Do not tell me how much to take.
```

```
I read this claim about a supplement: <claim>. Using the evidence levels
above, tell me what kind of evidence would be needed to support it and how to
look it up on the NIH Office of Dietary Supplements site.
```

```
Help me prepare for a pharmacist visit. I take these medicines: <list> and
these supplements: <list>. Write the questions I should ask about
interactions, without giving me any doses.
```
